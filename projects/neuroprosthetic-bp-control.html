Does a Blood-Pressure Neuroprosthesis Need a Smarter Controller, or Just a Posture Sensor?

In 20 held-out virtual patients with spinal cord injury, giving the clinical PID controller a posture signal cut the time systolic blood pressure spent above 150 mmHg by about 85 %. A model-based controller added little, and the benefit disappears when the posture sensor fails.

The problem

After a spinal cord injury high in the neck or upper back, the nerve pathways that tighten blood vessels when a person sits up no longer work. When they are tilted or sat upright, blood pools in the legs and belly and blood pressure falls, sometimes enough to faint (orthostatic hypotension).

Epidural stimulation of the lower spinal cord can switch those circuits back on and raise blood pressure. Clinical teams now run it in a closed loop: a sensor measures blood pressure, and a controller adjusts the stimulation amplitude. The published controllers are a PI controller (Squair et al., Nature 2021), a PID controller on a purpose-built implant (Phillips et al., Nature Medicine 2025) and a rule-based controller with timers (Christie et al., medRxiv 2026). All of them react only after blood pressure has already changed. In the Christie study, every tilt pushed systolic pressure above 150 mmHg.

None of these studies identifies a model of how blood pressure responds to stimulation, and none compares controller designs on the same patients. This project asks, in simulation: would a model-based controller, or simply knowing the patient's posture, make the system safer?

What I built

  • A virtual-patient simulator. CVSim, an open 21-compartment model of the heart and circulation with baroreflex control (Heldt et al., PhysioNet), compiled from its own C code with three small patches: an external tilt profile, and two hooks through which stimulation acts on vessel resistance and venous volume.
  • Checked against people. The healthy model was calibrated to head-up tilt recordings from 10 healthy adults (PRCP database, PhysioNet): early mean-pressure drop after rapid tilt −10.5 vs −9.0 mmHg, heart-rate peak +16.0 vs +15.2 bpm. The injury model (sympathetic control cut, lower vascular tone, reset baroreflex) was fitted to tilt responses after complete cervical injury reported by Corbett et al. (1971).
  • A stimulation actuator with delay, lag and a linear amplitude–pressure gain (Phillips et al. report a linear relationship). The delay and lag have not been published, so they were varied across patients (2–12 s delay, 5–30 s lag) rather than assumed.
  • 60 virtual patients with different physiology and stimulation response: 40 for tuning, 20 held out for testing. Each runs a 30-minute session with a 60° tilt, a return to lying, a 30° tilt, a dysreflexia-like pressure surge the controller cannot see, sensor dropouts and measurement noise.
  • Seven controller types: rule-based with timers (after Christie et al.), PID with and without a measurement filter, LQI and model-predictive control (MPC), each with and without posture feedforward (the tilt angle from a wearable sensor). Every type was tuned by Bayesian optimisation with the same budget. The model-based controllers never see the true patient: they use a model identified from a simulated 5–60-minute calibration session.
  • Decision rules written before the runs, including a 30 % improvement threshold over the tuned PID and a robustness test over 15 conditions.

Results

  • Posture is what matters, not controller sophistication. Adding the posture signal to the clinical PID gave nearly the whole benefit (−85 % time above 150 mmHg). MPC with posture reached −89 %; MPC without posture only −24 %.
  • Time in range barely moves. 56 % for the tuned clinical PID, 56–60 % for the LQI and MPC variants, 52 % for the tuned rule-based controller. Patients whose stimulation response is weak hit the amplitude limit during the 60° tilt whatever the controller does.
  • Tuning matters more than people assume. An untuned PID reacting to raw, noisy pressure changed the amplitude about 900 times per session; with a simple measurement filter and tuning, about 240.
  • Model-based control adds some robustness. MPC with posture stayed lower than PID with posture under faster tilt-down, a 30 % stronger stimulation response and long sensor dropouts, and made about half as many amplitude changes (198 vs 376 per session).
  • Calibration is cheap. A 5–10-minute calibration gave the full benefit. The identified model was imperfect (longer sessions fit the slow fluid shift worse), but the closed loop did not care.

When does it break?

The pre-specified robustness test (at least 50 % benefit in every condition) failed, because of the frozen-sensor case. The failure is graceful: the posture controllers fall back to roughly plain-PID behaviour and were never worse than it on time above 150 mmHg. A real device would still need posture-sensor fault detection.

How far to trust the simulator

Limitations

  • Everything is simulated. The results rank controller designs; they do not predict what any patient would experience.
  • The spinal-cord-injury model is fitted to one 1971 study, and the stimulation dynamics are assumed and varied, not measured.
  • The model understates the clinical problem: the tuned PID was above 150 mmHg for about 3 % of the return-to-lying phase, whereas Christie et al. saw every tilt cross it. Absolute hypertensive time is small, so the relative reductions rest on short episodes.
  • Return to lying, where the effect lives, is the least validated part of the model. It was stress-tested (tilt-down speed, fluid return), and the effect held, but it has not been checked against human data.
  • Posture feedforward raises the pressure peak at tilt-up (about 125 vs 117 mmHg on average) because it stimulates ahead of the fall.
  • No human stimulation data were available. A real-data system-identification arm is the obvious next step.

Process notes

The decision rules were written down before each set of runs. Two fairness problems were found in the first comparison: the PID's tuning stopped early, and it acted on unfiltered pressure while the model-based controllers had a filter. Both favoured my own method; both were fixed before the final comparison, and the stronger PID became the reference. The method follows the RWTH Aachen Learning-based Control course (LQR, learned dynamics, Bayesian tuning, MPC).
